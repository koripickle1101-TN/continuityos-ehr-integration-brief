# Risk Mitigation Plan

## Risk 1: Resistance to Change

Staff may resist a new or optimized information system if it feels like another burden. This can be reduced by involving frontline users early, explaining the purpose of the change, and using clinical and operational champions.

## Risk 2: Temporary Productivity Loss

Productivity may drop during training and go-live. This can be reduced through phased implementation, realistic workflow testing, super-user support, and at-the-elbow guidance.

## Risk 3: Alert Fatigue

Poorly configured alerts can overwhelm clinicians and reduce trust in the system. Alerts should be limited, meaningful, role-specific, and reviewed after go-live.

## Risk 4: Workflow Mismatch

The system may not match how care is actually delivered. This can be reduced by mapping real workflows before configuration and validating workflows with staff before launch.

## Risk 5: Revenue Cycle Disruption

Coding, charge capture, and billing workflows may be temporarily disrupted during implementation. This can be reduced by testing claims workflows, monitoring denial trends, and creating a rapid issue-escalation process.

## Risk 6: Patient Confusion

Patients may experience confusion if registration, portal communication, or billing processes change without clear explanation. This can be reduced through patient-facing communication, simple instructions, and consistent staff messaging.

## Risk 7: Data Quality Problems

Bad data can move through the system faster if upstream intake errors are not addressed. This can be reduced through front-end validation, documentation completeness checks, and clear ownership of data correction workflows.

## Mitigation Strategy Summary

A successful implementation requires workflow design, staff involvement, patient-centered communication, phased deployment, training, and continuous monitoring after go-live.
